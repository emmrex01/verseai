# Chapter 1: The Postcard

The postcard arrived on a Tuesday, wedged between a gas bill and a flyer for a pizza place that had closed in the spring. Sarah Mitchell turned it over twice before she understood what she was holding. The picture showed the Eiffel Tower under a bruised purple sky. The back was blank except for a single line in her brother's narrow handwriting: *Don't look for me.*

She sat down on the bottom stair of the apartment building and read the line again. Michael had been gone for nineteen days. The police had stopped returning her calls after the first week.

"You okay?" Mrs. Albright from 2B was standing over her with a bag of groceries.

"Fine," Sarah said. She was thirty-four years old and she had learned long ago that "fine" was a door you could close in anyone's face.

Upstairs, she set the postcard on the kitchen table and stared at it under the yellow light. Her own reflection looked back at her from the dark window — the same sharp chin as their father, the same bright green eyes that everyone said were her best feature. Michael had the eyes too. He was the younger one, three years behind her, thirty-one now, and he had never once in his life done what he was told.

She called Daniel Reyes because there was no one else to call.

"It's from Paris," she told him. "The postmark is smudged, but it's a French stamp."

"Paris," Daniel repeated. There was a long pause on the line. "Have you ever been?"

"I've never been to Paris," Sarah said. "I've never been anywhere. Michael was the one who wanted to travel. I stayed here and paid his rent."

Daniel had been Michael's best friend since high school. He drove a battered red pickup truck with a dent in the passenger door, and he had been the last person to see Michael before he vanished. He said so himself, every time anyone asked.

"I'll come over," he said.

When he arrived, rain was beating against the windows. He shook the water from his jacket and looked at the postcard for a long time without touching it.

"Where were you the night he left?" Sarah asked. She had asked it before. She would probably ask it again.

"Home," Daniel said. "I told the police. I was home all night."

Before he left, Sarah went to the drawer in the hall and took out the one thing their grandfather had left them: a silver pocket watch with an inscription inside the lid. Grandpa had given it to Michael on his twenty-first birthday. Michael had left it behind, which was the strangest thing of all. He never went anywhere without it.

She put the watch in her coat pocket. If she found her brother, she wanted to give it back to him herself.

# Chapter 2: The Letter

On the morning of March 10th, a second message came. This time it was a letter, in a long cream-colored envelope, and it was addressed to Sarah in the same narrow hand.

She noticed the postmark before she opened it: March 12th, Lyon.

The letter was short. Michael wrote that he was safe, that he had made a mistake, that he was sorry about the money. He wrote that someone had been following him since the night at the station, and that she should not trust anyone who claimed to have been with him that night.

Sarah read the last sentence four times.

She drove to Daniel's house in the late afternoon. His red pickup was parked crooked in the driveway, one wheel on the lawn. She found him in the garage, sorting through boxes of old records.

"Michael says someone was with him at the station," she said, holding up the letter. "The night he left."

Daniel's hands stopped moving. "What station?"

"Union Station. You know which one."

He didn't answer for a while. Then he sat down on an overturned crate and rubbed his face with both hands.

"Okay," he said. "I lied. I was there. I drove him to the station that night and I watched him get on the train. He made me promise not to tell anyone. He said if I told, they'd find him."

"Who's they?"

"I don't know. I swear I don't know."

Sarah wanted to hit him. Instead she sat down on a crate across from him, and for the first time in nineteen days she cried.

When she got home, she called the number for the Lyon post office that she found online, but the woman who answered spoke no English, and Sarah's high-school French deserted her completely.

She sat at the kitchen table until midnight, turning the letter over and over, the silver watch ticking in her coat pocket on the back of the chair.

# Chapter 3: The Hotel

Three days later, Sarah dyed her hair black in the bathroom sink. It had always been a soft brown, but she wanted to look like someone else, and she had read once that the first thing people remembered about a stranger was the color of their hair.

She bought a plane ticket with the last of her savings.

Detective Laura Okafor met her at a coffee shop near the airport. Okafor was the only officer who had ever seemed to take Michael's disappearance seriously.

"You're really going," Okafor said.

"He's my brother."

"Your older brother can take care of himself," Okafor said, and when Sarah frowned, the detective added, "That's what you told me the first day. That Michael, older by two years, had always looked out for you, not the other way around."

Sarah didn't remember saying that. She didn't remember much about that first day.

Okafor slid a folder across the table. Inside were photographs from a security camera at Union Station: grainy, black-and-white, timestamped 11:42 p.m. In one of them, Michael was walking toward the platform. A few steps behind him, a man in a dark jacket was watching.

"That's not Daniel," Sarah said.

"No," Okafor agreed. "We don't know who that is."

Sarah looked at the photograph for a long time. The man's face was turned away, but his hand was raised, and in it was something small and round and silver that caught the light.

She reached into her coat pocket. The watch was still there, ticking. Whatever the stranger was holding, it was not her grandfather's watch.

Her own eyes looked back at her from the window of the coffee shop, dark brown and tired, and she barely recognized herself with the black hair.

# Chapter 4: Montmartre

Paris was colder than she expected. Sarah walked up the hill to Montmartre in the gray morning and found, to her surprise, that her feet knew the way.

Two years in that cramped Montmartre flat after college had taught her to ignore the church bells and to buy bread before seven, when the baker on the corner still had the good loaves. She had loved it here once. She had come home when their father got sick, and she had never gone back.

She found the hotel from the postcard's return address — the Hôtel des Abbesses — and asked the clerk about her brother.

"Michel?" the clerk said. "Yes. He stayed here. He left four days ago."

"Did he leave anything?"

The clerk disappeared into a back room and came back with a small envelope. Inside was a key and a note: *Daniel knows more than he told you.*

Sarah's phone buzzed. It was a message from Daniel: *I'm here. I flew in last night. I'm sorry. I need to tell you the rest.*

She met him in a café on the Place des Abbesses. He looked exhausted. He had rented a car at the airport, he said, because he couldn't stand the trains anymore, and he had driven straight into the city.

"Back home I could never afford anything but that old blue sedan," he said, stirring his coffee. "It's the only car I've ever owned. And now I'm renting a Peugeot in Paris like a rich man."

"Tell me the rest," Sarah said.

Daniel told her about the money. Michael had borrowed forty thousand dollars from a man named Victor Crane to start a business, and the business had failed, and Crane wanted his money back. Daniel had introduced them.

"The man at the station," Sarah said. "Was it Crane?"

"I think so."

Sarah took the silver watch out of her pocket and set it on the table between them. Daniel stared at it.

"Where did you get that?" he asked.

"Michael left it behind."

"No," Daniel said slowly. "He didn't. He had it at the station. I saw him check the time on it right before he got on the train."

They looked at each other across the table, and somewhere above them, the bells began to ring.
